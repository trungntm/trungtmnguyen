# Research Framework

Research exists to improve engineering understanding, not to maximize information.

The objective is not to collect facts.

The objective is to discover the ideas that help software engineers make better decisions.

Research broadly.

Write selectively.

---

## Start with Questions

Before researching a topic, identify the questions the article should answer.

Typical questions include:

- What problem does this solve?
- Why does this problem exist?
- Why was this solution designed this way?
- What alternatives exist?
- What tradeoffs does it introduce?
- When should engineers choose it?
- When should they avoid it?
- What mistakes do teams commonly make?
- What lessons apply beyond this technology?

Research should answer questions, not collect facts.

---

## Understand the Problem First

Before explaining a solution, understand the problem it addresses.

Research:

- historical context
- engineering constraints
- previous approaches
- limitations of existing solutions
- why change became necessary

A reader who understands the problem will understand the solution much more easily.

---

## Discover the Design Rationale

Do not stop at learning how something works.

Investigate:

- why it was designed this way
- which alternatives were considered
- what assumptions influenced the design
- which tradeoffs were accepted

Understanding design decisions is usually more valuable than understanding implementation details.

---

## Research Tradeoffs

Every engineering decision has tradeoffs.

Whenever researching a technology, pattern, or architecture, identify:

- advantages
- disadvantages
- operational costs
- maintenance implications
- scalability considerations
- performance characteristics
- developer experience
- security implications

Avoid presenting technologies as universally superior.

---

## Research Production Usage

Move beyond tutorials and examples.

Investigate how the topic behaves in production.

Whenever appropriate, research topics such as:

- deployment
- testing
- observability
- debugging
- monitoring
- operations
- maintainability
- compatibility
- migration
- scalability
- failure recovery

Readers should understand what happens after software reaches production.

---

## Research Alternatives

Whenever practical, identify realistic alternatives.

For each alternative, understand:

- where it performs well
- where it struggles
- what assumptions it makes
- why teams choose it
- why teams reject it

The goal is not to find a winner.

The goal is to help readers make informed engineering decisions.

---

## Research Common Mistakes

Look for recurring implementation mistakes.

Examples include:

- common misconceptions
- incorrect assumptions
- architectural mistakes
- operational failures
- deployment issues
- maintenance problems
- performance bottlenecks

Explain:

- why they happen
- how to recognize them
- how to avoid them

The most valuable lessons often come from failures rather than success stories.

---

## Research Best Practices

Best practices should never become a list of rules.

For every recommendation, understand:

- what problem it solves
- when it applies
- when it does not
- why experienced engineers recommend it

Never recommend a practice without understanding its reasoning.

---

## Research the Ecosystem

Understand where the topic fits within the broader ecosystem.

Research:

- related technologies
- competing approaches
- complementary tools
- industry adoption
- ecosystem maturity
- future direction

The objective is to help readers understand the bigger picture rather than an isolated technology.

---

## Discover Insights

Do not stop after collecting information.

Actively search for insights.

Ask questions such as:

- What surprised me during research?
- What misconception appears repeatedly?
- What engineering lesson extends beyond this topic?
- What pattern appears in multiple technologies?
- What idea would experienced engineers likely emphasize?
- What decision framework can readers reuse?

Facts explain a topic.

Insights change how readers think about it.

---

## Synthesize Before Writing

Research should not become the article.

Before writing:

- identify recurring themes
- connect related ideas
- remove duplicated information
- prioritize the strongest insights
- organize ideas around the central argument

Readers should encounter a coherent explanation rather than the order in which the research was performed.

Research gathers information.

Synthesis creates understanding.

---

## Build Mental Models

Look for ways to simplify complex ideas.

Research should identify:

- analogies
- comparisons
- layered models
- decision trees
- responsibility boundaries
- lifecycle models
- architectural patterns

A good mental model often provides more long-term value than a detailed explanation.

---

## Select Research Carefully

Research broadly.

Write selectively.

Include information only when it:

- supports the central argument
- explains an engineering decision
- provides evidence
- reveals a meaningful tradeoff
- corrects an important misconception
- strengthens a mental model
- improves practical decision-making

Do not include information simply because it is interesting.

Every paragraph should help readers understand the topic more deeply.

---

## Use Primary Sources

Whenever possible, prefer:

- official documentation
- standards
- specifications
- RFCs
- official announcements
- source code
- source repositories
- original research papers
- engineering blog posts written by project maintainers

Avoid relying exclusively on secondary summaries.

Verify important claims against primary sources whenever practical.

---

## Prioritize Sources

Not all sources carry the same weight.

Whenever possible, research in this order:

1. Official documentation
2. Standards and specifications
3. Source code
4. Engineering blogs written by project maintainers
5. Conference talks
6. Reputable engineering blogs
7. Community discussions

Use lower-priority sources to discover ideas.

Use higher-priority sources to verify them.

When multiple trustworthy sources disagree, explain the disagreement rather than selecting one without justification.

---

## Evidence and Citations

Provide citations whenever appropriate for:

- official behavior
- version-specific features
- governance
- benchmarks
- statistics
- adoption numbers
- research findings
- time-sensitive information

Support recommendations with evidence rather than confidence.

When evidence is mixed, explain why.

Do not hide uncertainty.

---

## Verify Current Information

For rapidly evolving topics, verify that the information is still current before writing.

Examples include:

- AI models
- framework versions
- cloud services
- official recommendations
- pricing
- benchmarks
- industry best practices

Do not rely solely on existing knowledge when freshness materially affects correctness.

When current information cannot be verified, clearly state the uncertainty rather than presenting assumptions as facts.

---

## Generalize Beyond the Topic

Look for engineering lessons that apply outside the current technology.

For example:

- architectural principles
- design patterns
- operational lessons
- decision frameworks
- recurring tradeoffs

A great article should improve the reader's overall engineering judgment, not merely their knowledge of one technology.

---

## Stop Researching

More research does not automatically create a better article.

Stop researching when:

- the central questions have been answered
- the main tradeoffs are understood
- the engineering decisions are clear
- enough evidence supports the conclusions
- additional information adds breadth but not insight

The goal is not exhaustive coverage.

The goal is sufficient understanding to teach the topic with confidence.

---

## Research Mindset

Research is not information gathering.

Research is the process of discovering:

- why something exists
- how engineers make decisions
- what tradeoffs matter
- what lessons are transferable
- what ideas readers will remember

Collect facts.

Extract insights.

Teach understanding.
