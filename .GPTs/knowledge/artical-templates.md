# Preferred Article Structures

Choose the structure that best serves the topic.

These are editorial frameworks, not rigid templates.

Adapt, merge, reorder, or omit sections whenever doing so improves the article.

Every article should revolve around one central argument.

Do not include sections simply because they appear in a template.

---

## Tutorial

Use when teaching implementation, workflows, or hands-on engineering tasks.

Typical structure:

- Hook
- The Problem
- Background
- The Solution
- Step-by-Step Walkthrough
- Why This Works
- Production Considerations
- Best Practices
- Common Mistakes
- Summary
- What's Next

The objective is not merely to teach readers how to complete a task.

Help them understand why each step exists and what could go wrong in production.

---

## Deep Dive

Use for explaining technologies, architectures, engineering concepts, design decisions, or engineering practices.

Typical structure:

- Hook
- The Problem
- Why It Exists
- The Core Idea
- How It Works
- Design Decisions
- Tradeoffs
- Production Considerations
- Common Misconceptions
- Practical Recommendations
- Conclusion

Optional sections:

- Historical Context
- Internal Architecture
- Performance Analysis
- Security Considerations
- Scaling Considerations
- Ecosystem Comparison
- Future Direction

Include optional sections only when they strengthen the article.

Do not expand the article simply because more information is available.

---

## Comparison

Use when evaluating multiple technologies, tools, frameworks, or architectural approaches.

Typical structure:

- The Problem
- Evaluation Criteria
- Option A
- Option B
- Comparison Table
- Tradeoffs
- Production Considerations
- Recommendation

Do not declare universal winners.

Help readers choose the right solution based on their context.

---

## Best Practices

Use when discussing engineering guidelines, operational practices, conventions, or architectural recommendations.

Typical structure:

- Why This Matters
- Common Approaches
- Recommended Practices
- Tradeoffs
- Common Mistakes
- Production Lessons
- Summary

Every recommendation should explain:

- why it exists
- when it applies
- when it should not be followed

Avoid presenting preferences as universal rules.

---

## Opinion

Use when discussing engineering philosophies, architectural viewpoints, or long-term industry trends.

Typical structure:

- Context
- The Core Argument
- Supporting Evidence
- Alternative Viewpoints
- Tradeoffs
- Conclusion

Opinions should always be supported by engineering reasoning rather than personal preference.

The goal is to encourage thoughtful discussion rather than persuade readers emotionally.

---

## Case Study

Use when analyzing real engineering work, production incidents, migrations, or architectural decisions.

Typical structure:

- Background
- Problem
- Constraints
- Decision Process
- Solution
- Results
- Lessons Learned
- What We Would Do Differently

Focus on engineering decisions and their consequences.

The lesson should always be more valuable than the story itself.

---

## Reference Guide

Use for topics where readers are likely to revisit the article repeatedly.

Examples include:

- Terraform commands
- Git commands
- Docker Compose examples
- Spring annotations
- Kubernetes resources

Typical structure:

- Overview
- Quick Reference
- Detailed Explanations
- Examples
- Common Pitfalls
- Related Topics

Optimize for clarity and discoverability.

Do not turn every article into a reference guide.

---

## Choosing the Right Structure

Do not force every topic into the same structure.

Some articles naturally combine multiple frameworks.

Examples:

- Deep Dive + Comparison
- Tutorial + Best Practices
- Case Study + Opinion
- Deep Dive + Case Study

Always choose the structure that best supports the central argument.

---

## Editorial Rules

Regardless of structure:

- Begin with the engineering problem before introducing the solution.
- Explain WHY before HOW.
- Introduce the central insight early.
- Discuss tradeoffs honestly.
- Include production considerations whenever appropriate.
- End with practical guidance or a memorable takeaway.

Readers should leave with:

- a better understanding
- a reusable mental model
- improved engineering judgment
- greater confidence when making similar decisions in the future
