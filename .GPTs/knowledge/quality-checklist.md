# Quality Checklist

The objective is not simply to produce a correct article.

The objective is to produce an article that software engineers genuinely find valuable, memorable, and worth sharing.

Before returning the final article, review it critically using the checklist below.

If multiple items fail, revise the article before publishing.

---

## 1. Technical Accuracy

Verify that:

- Every technical statement is accurate.
- Recommendations are technically justified.
- Assumptions are clearly stated.
- Version-specific behavior is identified when relevant.
- Time-sensitive information has been verified.
- Examples are technically correct.

Never sacrifice correctness for readability.

When uncertain, state the uncertainty instead of guessing.

---

## 2. Central Argument

A great article revolves around one memorable idea.

Verify that:

- The article has one clear central argument.
- Every major section supports that argument.
- The conclusion reinforces the same idea.
- No section feels disconnected from the overall narrative.

If readers remember only one thing after reading the article, it should be obvious what that thing is.

---

## 3. Reader Value

Ask yourself:

"What will readers know, understand, or do differently after reading this article?"

Every major section should provide meaningful value.

Avoid sections that merely:

- define terminology
- summarize documentation
- repeat obvious information
- list features without explanation

Every section should justify the reader's time.

---

## 4. Engineering Thinking

Verify that the article explains:

- why something exists
- what problem it solves
- why this design was chosen
- tradeoffs
- alternatives
- production implications
- operational impact
- long-term maintenance considerations

Engineering articles should improve decision-making rather than simply explain technologies.

---

## 5. Depth

Depth does not mean writing longer articles.

Verify that the article explores:

- motivations
- assumptions
- tradeoffs
- consequences
- failure modes
- engineering decisions

Avoid replacing depth with exhaustive coverage.

Prefer focused insight over encyclopedic completeness.

---

## 6. Originality

Ask yourself:

Could this article have been produced by any generic AI assistant?

If the answer is yes, improve it.

Increase originality by adding:

- engineering insights
- design rationale
- practical lessons
- production experience
- useful comparisons
- reusable mental models
- decision frameworks

The goal is to contribute original thinking rather than reorganize existing information.

---

## 7. Insight

Every article should teach at least one memorable idea.

Ask:

- What is the biggest misconception?
- What surprised me during research?
- What idea changes how readers think?
- What engineering lesson extends beyond this topic?
- What practical framework can readers reuse?

Readers rarely remember every section.

They remember meaningful insights.

---

## 8. Mental Models

Whenever appropriate, verify that the article includes at least one of the following:

- a mental model
- a useful analogy
- a comparison
- a decision tree
- a layered model
- a reusable framework

A memorable mental model often teaches more effectively than pages of explanation.

---

## 9. Research Quality

Verify that:

- Research supports the central argument.
- Important claims are supported by evidence.
- Time-sensitive information is verified.
- Unnecessary facts have been removed.
- Research improves understanding rather than increasing article length.

Research should answer engineering questions.

It should not become an information dump.

---

## 10. Production Value

Whenever appropriate, verify that the article discusses:

- deployment
- testing
- observability
- operations
- maintainability
- scalability
- compatibility
- security
- operational risks

Readers should understand how the topic behaves in production, not only in examples.

---

## 11. Examples

Verify that examples are:

- realistic
- technically accurate
- production-inspired
- easy to understand
- directly related to the discussion

Avoid:

- toy examples
- unrealistic code
- artificial scenarios
- examples that merely demonstrate syntax

Examples should strengthen understanding, not fill space.

---

## 12. Readability

Verify that:

- paragraphs are concise
- headings are meaningful
- transitions feel natural
- terminology is consistent
- repetition has been removed

Avoid:

- filler
- unnecessary adjectives
- marketing language
- obvious statements
- repetitive explanations

A technically deep article should still be enjoyable to read.

---

## 13. Focus

Verify that:

- no two sections teach substantially the same lesson
- overlapping sections have been merged
- every example introduces new understanding
- optional content has been removed when it does not strengthen the article

Remove information that is technically correct but unnecessary.

A focused article is usually stronger than a longer article.

---

## 14. Search & AI Discoverability

Verify that:

- the article satisfies a clear search intent
- headings accurately describe their sections
- keywords appear naturally
- metadata is concise and descriptive
- related topics can be linked internally
- the conclusion reinforces the central idea

Write for software engineers first.

Optimize for search engines and AI systems second.

---

## 15. Citations

Verify that citations exist whenever appropriate for:

- official behavior
- specifications
- governance
- research findings
- benchmarks
- statistics
- adoption numbers
- version-specific information
- time-sensitive claims

Prefer primary sources whenever possible.

Evidence should support recommendations.

---

## 16. Editorial Compression

Perform one final editing pass.

Ask:

- Can two sections be merged?
- Can this explanation become shorter without losing value?
- Is there unnecessary repetition?
- Can a list become a framework?
- Can an example teach multiple ideas?
- Does every paragraph introduce something new?

Do not optimize for article length.

Optimize for information density.

---

## 17. Memorability

Before publishing, ask:

- What is the one idea readers will remember?
- Is that idea introduced early enough?
- Is it reinforced throughout the article?
- Does the conclusion leave readers with that same idea?

If the article has many facts but no memorable insight, strengthen the editorial narrative.

---

## Final Review

Before returning the article, answer these questions honestly.

- Would I recommend this article to another software engineer?
- Would I bookmark it?
- Would I reference it in the future?
- Would I share it with my team?
- Does every section earn its place?
- Does the article improve engineering judgment?
- Does it teach more "why" than "how"?
- Does it contain at least one memorable insight?
- Does it contain at least one reusable mental model?
- Does it provide at least one practical decision framework?
- Does it avoid unnecessary completeness?
- Is it shorter than it could be without losing value?
- Could this article have been written by any generic AI assistant?

If the answer to the last question is **yes**, revise the article before publishing.
